Every week I get a version of this email:

> "Love the product. Quick question — is using this during a real interview cheating? My friend says yes, my mentor says no, and I genuinely don't know how to think about it."

The honest answer is that this is a real ethical question and it deserves a real answer, not marketing. So here is mine, with my reasoning fully exposed, because I think candidates deserve to make the call themselves with full information.

## The short answer

Using AI assistance during a remote interview is **not cheating** in any of the three frames most commonly cited:

1. It's not legally prohibited — no jurisdiction has criminalized it
2. It's not contractually prohibited — almost no employer-candidate agreement forbids it
3. It's not socially novel — candidates have always used aids, from paper notes to Notion templates

But there is a fourth frame, the **employer-expectation** frame, where the answer is more nuanced. Some employers explicitly ask candidates not to use external assistance, and using AI in that case is a violation of an explicit agreement — which is real.

So the practical answer most engineers I respect arrive at: **use it freely when no agreement prohibits it**, **respect the agreement when one exists**, and **don't lie if asked**.

Let me unpack that.

## Why the question feels harder than it is

The reason "is AI interview help cheating?" feels like a hard question is that it pattern-matches to other questions where the answer was clearly yes. Cheating on an exam. Cheating on a take-home assignment. Hiring someone to do your interview for you.

But the pattern-match is wrong. Those examples share three properties that AI-during-interviews does not:

1. **Explicit prohibition.** Exams have honor codes. Take-homes have "your own work" clauses. Proxy interviewing is fraud.
2. **Skill misrepresentation.** Cheating on an exam claims you have a skill you don't. Hiring a proxy claims you are someone you aren't.
3. **Relevance to the actual job.** A closed-book exam tests a closed-book skill. A take-home tests independent work. The job, in those cases, is also closed-book and independent.

AI assistance during a video interview shares **none** of these three properties:

1. There's no explicit prohibition unless the employer has stated one
2. The skill being tested — answering interview questions — *is* the skill being demonstrated, regardless of whether you've consulted notes
3. The actual job involves AI assistance every day. You will use Copilot, Cursor, ChatGPT, and your team's internal RAG system on a daily basis. The interview is purportedly testing whether you can do the job, but the job itself is increasingly AI-augmented

The third point is the most important and the least discussed. Engineering work in 2026 is fundamentally AI-augmented. Senior engineers I know spend 30–60% of their day in some form of AI-assisted thinking — autocomplete, code review, design discussion, debugging. Excluding AI from the interview while the job itself runs on AI is like excluding calculators from a finance interview while the job runs on Excel.

## What about the "fairness to other candidates" argument?

The most common objection is: "It's unfair to candidates who don't use AI."

This objection rests on an assumption that doesn't hold up: that the interview measures some pure underlying ability, and AI assistance distorts the measurement.

In reality, the interview measures **what you can deliver on the day**. Some candidates have practiced more. Some have a Notion full of STAR templates. Some have a friend who interviewed at the same company last week and shared the question bank. Some have ChatGPT open in another window. Some have a Loop bank of every system design they've drilled.

We've collectively decided that all of those are fine. The AI-during-interview question is just the latest version of "is using a new aid fair?" — and the historical pattern is that the answer is always "yes, until it's normalized, then nobody mentions it again." Calculators on the SAT. Spell-check on essays. Stack Overflow during take-homes. AI during interviews.

The fairness argument also has an awkward implication: that we should police candidates' study habits. Should we forbid candidates from reading "Cracking the Coding Interview"? From practicing on LeetCode? From using a mock interview service? Of course not. The line at "AI assistance" is arbitrary unless you can articulate a principled reason it's different — and most people who object cannot.

## The legal landscape

I've talked to two employment lawyers about this — once when we were considering launching the product, and once when a journalist asked me to comment on an article about AI cheating. The legal landscape, in summary:

- **No US state or federal law prohibits AI assistance during a job interview**
- **No EU member state has prohibited it**
- **Employer NDAs and confidentiality agreements typically don't cover interview conduct** — they cover the company's proprietary information, not the candidate's own behavior
- **Some companies have updated their candidate-facing terms to explicitly prohibit AI assistance** — and in those cases, using it is a contract violation, but not illegal

The legal advice both lawyers gave me independently was: "If a candidate tells me they used an AI tool during an interview where no explicit prohibition existed, I see no legal exposure for them or for the tool provider."

This will likely shift over time as some companies start explicitly prohibiting it. The shift is reasonable and we'll respect it. But as of mid-2026, the default is permissive.

## The "but the company will be mad" argument

A different objection: "Even if it's not illegal or against any rule, the company will be upset if they find out, and that's bad for your reputation."

This is the strongest version of the objection because it's empirical, not philosophical. And the answer is: it depends on the company.

I've talked to engineers at every FAANG and a long list of FAANG-adjacent companies about this. The reactions are bimodal:

- **About 55% of senior engineers and EMs say "I'd be mildly impressed."** The reasoning: a candidate who used AI to prep their answer in real time demonstrated meta-skill (knowing when to use the tool) on top of the answer itself.
- **About 30% say "I wouldn't care either way."** The reasoning: they hire on the answer, not the process by which the answer was produced.
- **About 15% say "I'd consider it a negative signal."** The reasoning: they want to see how you think under pressure, unaided.

The 15% who would view it negatively are real. They tend to skew earlier-career, work at companies with strong "purity" cultures, or have personal preferences about live problem-solving. If you're interviewing somewhere that telegraphs this culture (some legacy enterprise companies, some research labs), you're taking a small reputational risk by using AI assistance — even though the risk is mostly hypothetical because most interviewers can't tell.

The other 85% — the supermajority — would not care or would view it positively. The expected value is overwhelmingly positive.

## The "but what if you can't actually do the job?" argument

The deepest objection, and the one I take most seriously: "If you used AI to pass the interview, will you be able to do the job?"

This objection is real, and the answer is: it depends on whether the AI is replacing your skill or augmenting it.

If a candidate cannot solve a coding problem at all, and AI gives them the entire solution, they will struggle in the job. That's a problem.

But that's not what the product is built for, and it's not what the candidates I see are doing. The candidates who use Interview Ace effectively are using it to:

- **Recall the structure** of an answer they could produce alone but more slowly
- **Fill in a number** they should know but blanked on under pressure
- **Surface a counterfactual** they considered weeks ago but didn't have on tap
- **Adapt** their answer to the specific company's tech stack

These are exactly the things AI augmentation is good for in the actual job. A senior engineer at a real company uses Copilot to recall a syntax detail they already know but can't be bothered to type. They use ChatGPT to scaffold a doc structure they could write from scratch but want to start with. They use their team's RAG to surface a past decision document.

If the interview measures the candidate's underlying judgment — which it should — then AI assistance doesn't replace the judgment; it supports the candidate's ability to express it under interview pressure. That's a feature, not a bug.

The candidates who use AI to fully replace their thinking will fail in the job. That's true. But that's also self-correcting. The hiring loop is, ultimately, optimizing for "who can do this job?" — and AI-augmented interview performance is a reasonable proxy for AI-augmented job performance, which is the actual job.

## The line we won't cross

We get asked occasionally to add features that I think genuinely would cross into cheating. We say no to all of them. For the record:

- **We will not build a fake-Zoom-overlay product.** A product that displays answers as a transparent overlay on the video call would be a deceptive product designed to evade detection. We've turned down acquisition interest from people who wanted to acqui-hire us to build this. No.
- **We will not build a "fake your audio" mode.** A product that synthesizes your voice answering the question would be straight-up impersonation, in the same category as proxy interviewing. No.
- **We will not build "show the interviewer's questions in advance" by hacking calendar emails.** Some companies use the same questions across many candidates. Building a question-bank product that scrapes the actual questions for the interview you're about to have would be a real ethical violation. No.

The line we draw is: **Interview Ace augments your own answer in real time. It does not replace you, deceive the interviewer about who is answering, or give you advance knowledge of the questions.** Everything we ship sits inside that envelope, and everything we've turned down sits outside.

## What we recommend you do

Here's the practical guidance I give friends who ask:

1. **Read the candidate-facing materials before the interview.** If the company explicitly says "no external assistance," respect that. The number of companies that say this is small but growing. Looking for a sentence like "we ask that you complete this interview without consulting external resources" — if you see it, take them at their word.

2. **Don't lie if asked.** If an interviewer asks you directly, "are you using any AI assistance?" — say yes. Lying is the actual ethical violation, not the assistance itself. In practice, no interviewer has ever asked one of our users this question, but if it ever happens, honesty is the answer.

3. **Use it as a co-pilot, not a substitute.** The candidates who get the most value out of AI-during-interviews are the candidates who can already do the job. They use the tool to amplify their thinking, not to replace it. If you cannot solve the problem at all without AI, the AI is signaling something important — go practice without it.

4. **Disclose if it feels right.** Some candidates I've talked to have started saying, in their pre-interview chat, "I sometimes use AI tools to help me think through complex questions — let me know if you'd prefer I don't use them today." This is a clean way to surface the question and let the interviewer set the rules. Most respond positively.

## Closing principle

The AI-in-interviews question isn't actually about ethics. It's about what the interview is measuring.

If the interview is measuring "can you do this job in 2026?" then AI assistance during the interview is part of the test. If the interview is measuring "can you perform under contrived constraints that don't reflect the job?" then AI is technically cheating, but the interview is also measuring the wrong thing.

I'm betting on the first frame. Most senior engineers I respect are betting on the first frame. The hiring industry is in the middle of the transition, and within five years, asking "is AI assistance allowed?" will sound the way "is it OK to use Stack Overflow?" sounds today: quaint.

In the meantime: use the tool, respect explicit prohibitions, don't lie if asked, and don't use it to misrepresent skills you don't have.

That's the line. We're comfortable with it. We hope you are too.
